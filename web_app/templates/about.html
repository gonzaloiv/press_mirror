How {{ app_name }} works

What {{ app_name }} is

{{ app_name }} groups news articles from different outlets that cover the same event. Each card on the homepage is one event, as told by each outlet that published a story about it. The map shows which countries covered that event — and which ones didn't.

How the grouping works

Every day, {{ app_name }} collects headlines from a fixed set of European news outlets. It compares each headline with headlines from other outlets published that day, to see whether they are about the same event. Headlines about the same event are grouped into a cluster. When one outlet published several stories about the same event, {{ app_name }} keeps a single one — the story closest to what every outlet was saying — so each outlet appears once per event.

A cluster is the smallest unit of the product: one event, many voices. Everything else in the app — the map, the labels, the source list, the daily selection — is built on top of clusters.

The grouping compares the meaning of headlines across languages, not just their words. A French headline and an English headline about the same event can end up in the same cluster even when they share no vocabulary.

What the map shows

The map colors every country in one of three states:

Covered — we have sources from this country, and at least one published a story about this event.

Not covered by tracked sources — we have sources from this country, and none of them published a story about this event.

Not tracked — we don't have sources from this country. We make no claim about whether it covered the event.

The distinction between “not covered” and “not tracked” matters. When a country is gray, we know our sources there didn't cover the story. When it's outlined, we don't know — because we don't read its press. We don't claim a global view. We show a European view and where that view ends.

What each card shows

Each card on the homepage represents one event. The card shows:

  • A short label naming the event.
  • The shared terms its headlines have in common.
  • A map of the countries that covered it.
  • The number of outlets and countries involved.
  • The names of a few of those outlets.

On the detail page — reached by clicking any card — every outlet in the cluster is listed once, with one headline linked to the original article at its original outlet.

How the daily view is built

Each day, the pipeline produces a fixed set of stories — one per event. Only some reach the homepage: an event must be covered by at least six outlets from at least four countries, with headlines similar enough to be one story. Events that make the bar are ordered by how many countries covered them. The selection is designed to answer one question: what is Europe talking about today?

Some events appear on multiple days as they develop. Others appear once and fall away. The date navigation at the top of the homepage lets you browse back through previous days.

What we don't do

We don't rate bias or label outlets as left or right in the interface. We don't tell you what to think about the coverage we show.

We don't claim to be complete. If a country doesn't appear on a map, it may be because we don't have sources there — not because the event wasn't covered. Every headline links to the original article at its original outlet. To know what an outlet said, read its article.

We also don't generate the news. The short label and shared terms on each card are derived from the headlines; the shared terms are translated into English for display. This does not produce or edit journalism. It reflects what the press has already published.

What we do is show you, side by side, how different outlets told the same story. The comparison is yours to make.
